## Overview

Cargo insurance in road transport operates on two fundamentally different principles that are frequently confused by operators, shippers, and drivers:

1. **CMR Liability Insurance** — purchased by the **carrier** (transport operator); covers the carrier's legal liability under the CMR Convention for loss, damage, or delay to cargo. This covers liability up to the CMR limits.

2. **All-Risk Cargo Insurance (Goods in Transit Insurance)** — purchased by the **shipper or consignee**; covers the full commercial value of the goods regardless of who is at fault. This protects the shipper's own property interest.

These two types of cover serve different parties and different purposes. A shipper relying solely on the carrier's CMR insurance may be significantly underinsured if the cargo value exceeds 8.33 SDR per kilogram.

## CMR Liability Insurance

### What It Covers

CMR Liability Insurance covers the transport operator's (carrier's) **legal liability to the shipper or consignee** under the CMR Convention for:

- **Loss** of goods (total or partial)
- **Damage** to goods occurring between taking-over and delivery
- **Delay** in delivery (where delivery time was agreed or delay was unreasonable)

The insurance responds when the carrier is **liable** under CMR Article 17 — i.e., the carrier cannot establish one of the exemptions (inherent vice, force majeure, sender's fault, inadequate packing by sender).

### CMR Liability Limits

| Basis | Limit |
|-------|-------|
| Standard cargo loss/damage (Art. 23) | **8.33 SDR per kilogram** of gross weight of goods lost/damaged |
| Where sender declared higher value (Art. 24) | Full declared value (surcharge payable) |
| Delay (Art. 23(5)) | Up to the amount of **carriage charges** (freight) |
| Special interest in delivery declared (Art. 26) | Up to declared special interest amount |

*SDR conversion rate fluctuates — as of 2026, 1 SDR ≈ €1.25–1.35. At 8.33 SDR/kg, the limit is approximately €10–11 per kg.*

### What CMR Insurance Excludes

CMR liability insurance typically excludes or has sub-limits for:

| Exclusion | Reason |
|-----------|--------|
| Inherent vice of the goods | Art. 17(2) — carrier not liable |
| Inadequate packing by sender | Art. 17(4)(b) — special risk defence |
| Loss/damage caused solely by sender's instructions | Art. 17(2)(b) |
| Force majeure / circumstances carrier could not avoid | Art. 17(2)(d) |
| Wilful misconduct by carrier | Policy exclusion (and unlimited liability under Art. 29 CMR — separate issue) |
| Delay claims | Often sub-limited or excluded from standard CMR policies |
| Precious metals, currency, works of art | High-value exclusion — requires declaration and additional premium |
| Temperature-sensitive goods (if temperature control failure not proven as carrier fault) | Separate refrigerated cargo endorsement needed |

### CMR Insurance Premium and Structure

- **Premium basis:** Typically calculated as a percentage of annual **freight turnover** (transport revenue)
- **Typical rate:** 0.1–0.3% of annual turnover (varies by operator size, route profile, cargo types, claims history)
- **Example:** Operator with €5m annual freight turnover at 0.2% = €10,000/year CMR premium
- **Policy structure:** Annual aggregate limit (e.g., €2m or €5m per occurrence and in aggregate); some insurers offer per-consignment cover
- **Multi-modal:** CMR applies only to the **road leg**. If cargo moves sea-road or air-road, the carrier needs separate cover for non-road legs (or the freight forwarder arranges multimodal cover)

### CMR Article 29 — Unlimited Liability

If loss or damage results from the carrier's **wilful misconduct** or from default equivalent to wilful misconduct, the CMR liability limits do not apply — the carrier is liable for full actual value. Standard CMR policies typically **exclude** wilful misconduct (since it is uninsurable as a matter of public policy), meaning the carrier faces uninsured unlimited liability in such cases.

## All-Risk Cargo Insurance (Shipper's Cover)

### What It Is

All-Risk Cargo Insurance (also called Goods in Transit Insurance or Cargo Insurance) is purchased by the **owner of the goods** — the shipper, manufacturer, or sometimes the consignee — to protect the **commercial value** of the goods regardless of who caused the loss.

If goods worth €100,000 are destroyed and the carrier is liable, the carrier's CMR insurance pays up to the CMR limit (e.g., 8.33 SDR/kg × 10,000 kg = approximately €110,000 in this example — which might cover the full value). But if the carrier is not liable (inherent vice, force majeure), the shipper receives nothing from the carrier's insurance. The shipper's own cargo policy would respond.

### Institute Cargo Clauses (ICC)

The most widely used international cargo insurance standard is the **Institute Cargo Clauses** published by Lloyd's of London / the Institute of London Underwriters. Three levels of cover exist:

#### ICC-A (All Risks)

The broadest cover. Covers **all risks of loss or damage** to the insured goods except specifically excluded perils.

**Typical exclusions from ICC-A:**
- Wilful misconduct of the assured
- Ordinary leakage, ordinary loss in weight/volume, or ordinary wear and tear
- Inherent vice or nature of the subject matter
- Delay
- Insolvency or financial default of the carrier
- War (separate war clause available)
- Strikes, riots, civil commotions (separate strikes clause available)
- Deliberate damage/destruction by wrongful act of any person (terrorism — separate cover)

ICC-A is suitable for: high-value goods, fragile goods, electronics, pharmaceuticals, foodstuffs.

#### ICC-B (Named Perils — Broad)

Covers specific named perils only:
- Fire or explosion
- Vessel, craft, or vehicle being stranded, grounded, sunk, or capsized
- Overturning or derailment of land conveyance
- Collision or contact of vessel, craft, or conveyance with external object other than water
- Discharge of cargo at a port of distress
- Earthquake, volcanic eruption, or lightning
- General average sacrifice
- Jettison or washing overboard

ICC-B is a mid-level option — cheaper than ICC-A but misses some common land transport perils (e.g., theft, water damage from rain).

#### ICC-C (Named Perils — Minimum)

Covers only the most catastrophic perils — fire/explosion, stranding, collision/overturning, general average. Does not cover theft, water ingress, or most individual cargo damage.

ICC-C is rarely appropriate for road transport goods — it was designed primarily for sea bulk cargoes.

### Institute Strikes Clauses

A **separate endorsement** available alongside ICC-A, B, or C that covers loss or damage caused by:
- Strikers, locked-out workers, or persons taking part in labour disturbances
- Riots or civil commotions
- **Theft** is often bundled here or in an all-risks extension

For road transport of high-value or easily marketable goods (electronics, tobacco, spirits, pharmaceuticals), theft cover is critical and should be explicitly confirmed in the policy.

### Road Cargo Extensions to ICC

The ICC clauses were designed primarily for marine transport. Road cargo policies typically use ICC clauses as a base but may include road-specific extensions:
- Overturning and collision cover (already in ICC-B/C — confirmed for road vehicles)
- Loading and unloading risks
- Customs delay cover (where cargo is detained by customs)
- Driver dishonesty (employee theft)

## Temperature-Controlled Cargo

Standard CMR and ICC policies may **not** automatically cover temperature excursion losses. Specific issues:

| Issue | Coverage consideration |
|-------|----------------------|
| Refrigeration unit breakdown (carrier's fault) | CMR may respond if carrier is liable; ICC-A if shipper's own cover |
| Temperature excursion due to inherent vice (product sensitive above normal temp) | CMR excludes (Art. 17(4)(d)); ICC-A may exclude (inherent vice) |
| Pre-loading temperature failure (not carrier's fault) | Carrier not liable under CMR; needs shipper's own cover |
| Third-party reefer failure (ferry, rail leg) | CMR applies to road leg only; separate cover needed for non-road legs |

**Recommendation:** Shippers of perishable/temperature-controlled goods should ensure their cargo policy includes an explicit **temperature clause** covering refrigeration failure as a covered peril.

## FIATA Bill of Lading (FBL) — Freight Forwarder's Liability

When a **freight forwarder** (speditor) issues a **FIATA Multimodal Transport Bill of Lading (FBL)**, the forwarder acts as a **principal** (not merely an agent) and assumes carrier-like liability for the entire multimodal transport:

| Aspect | CMR Convention | FIATA FBL |
|--------|---------------|-----------|
| Applies to | Road leg of international carriage | Entire multimodal transport |
| Issued by | Carrier | Freight forwarder (acting as principal) |
| Liability limit (loss/damage) | 8.33 SDR/kg | 8.33 SDR/kg (same reference standard) |
| Liability limit (delay) | Freight amount | Freight amount |
| Governing law | CMR Convention | FIATA standard conditions |

Freight forwarders issuing FBLs must carry their own **freight forwarder's liability insurance** (FFL) to cover this exposure.

## Practical Decision Guide for Operators and Shippers

### Carrier (Transport Operator) Must Have:
1. **CMR Liability Insurance** — minimum requirement for any international road transport operator; verify coverage limits are adequate for typical consignment values
2. Certificate of insurance available to shippers on request
3. Separate temperature/refrigerated cargo endorsement if operating reefer vehicles

### Shipper Should Consider:
1. **Calculate the CMR limit** for each consignment: (gross weight in kg) × 8.33 SDR ≈ value of CMR coverage available
2. If the **commercial value exceeds the CMR limit** — purchase cargo insurance (ICC-A or equivalent) to cover the gap
3. If goods are high-risk for theft — ensure theft cover (Strikes Clauses or All-Risks)
4. For multimodal journeys — ensure cover extends to all legs or that separate policies are in place for sea/air segments

## Subcontracted Transport and Insurance

When the contracting carrier subcontracts to another carrier, the **contracting carrier remains liable** to the shipper under CMR Article 3 (see `references/eu/subcontracting-liability.md`). The contracting carrier's CMR insurance covers this exposure.

However, if the contracting carrier's insurer pays a claim and then exercises subrogation rights against the subcontractor, the subcontractor must have **their own CMR insurance** to meet that claim.

**Best practice:** Contracting carriers should:
- Verify the subcontractor holds valid CMR insurance
- Obtain a certificate of insurance showing adequate limits
- Include a back-to-back liability clause in the subcontract agreement

## Penalties

CMR itself does not prescribe penalties for absence of cargo insurance. However:

| Issue | Consequence |
|-------|------------|
| Carrier without CMR insurance causes cargo loss | Carrier faces uninsured CMR liability claim; may be unable to pay; can affect operator licence (financial standing requirement) |
| Shipper without cargo cover suffers uncompensated loss | No penalty, but commercial loss: CMR limits leave a significant gap for high-value goods |
| Freight forwarder issues FBL without FFL insurance | If cargo loss occurs and forwarder cannot pay, financial failure likely; regulatory consequences vary by country |
| Misrepresentation on CMR consignment note affecting insurance recovery | Insurer may avoid the policy; civil fraud consequences |
