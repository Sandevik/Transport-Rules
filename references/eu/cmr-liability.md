## Overview

The **Convention on the Contract for the International Carriage of Goods by Road (CMR)** is an international treaty governing liability and documentation for cross-border road freight transport. It was concluded in Geneva on 19 May 1956 and entered into force on 2 July 1961. The 1978 Geneva Protocol introduced SDR-based liability limits.

CMR applies to **every contract for the carriage of goods by road in vehicles for reward** when the place of taking over of the goods and the place designated for delivery are situated in two different countries, of which **at least one is a contracting party** to the Convention.

As of 2026, over 55 countries have ratified CMR, including all EU member states, EEA states, the UK, Turkey, Russia, and most other European and Central Asian nations.

---

## Scope of Application (Article 1)

CMR applies when:
- **Cross-border road transport**: goods carried by road vehicle for reward
- **At least one country is a CMR contracting state** (departure or destination)
- Applies regardless of the domicile or nationality of the parties

**Not covered by CMR:**
- Postal consignments under international postal conventions
- Funeral consignments
- Furniture removal transport

**Important:** CMR applies to **the entire journey** including where part is by sea, rail, or air — unless damage occurred during the non-road leg and a separate convention governs that leg.

## The CMR Consignment Note (Waybill) — Articles 4–9

### Article 4 — Evidence of Contract

The contract of carriage is established by a **consignment note**. Its absence, irregularity, or loss does not affect the validity or existence of the contract.

### Article 5 — Form and Contents

The consignment note is made out in **three original copies** signed by sender and carrier:
1. **First copy** — retained by the sender
2. **Second copy** — accompanies the goods (for recipient)
3. **Third copy** — retained by the carrier

**Mandatory particulars (Article 6):**

| Field | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| Date and place of issue | — |
| Name and address of sender | — |
| Name and address of carrier | — |
| Place and date of taking over | Where/when carrier received goods |
| Place designated for delivery | Destination |
| Name and address of consignee | Recipient |
| Description of goods | Nature, method of packing, marks/numbers |
| Number of packages | Count |
| Gross weight or quantity | In kg or other measurement |
| Charges relating to carriage | Freight, ancillary charges, customs duties |
| Instructions for Customs | If applicable |
| Statement that carriage is subject to CMR | — |

**Optional particulars (Article 6(2)):** Declared value, agreed delivery timeframe, list of documents handed to carrier, special agreements.

### Article 8 — Checking

On taking over the goods, the carrier must check:
- Accuracy of the statements in the consignment note relating to number, marks, and numbers of packages
- Apparent condition of the goods and packaging

If the carrier cannot verify, they must enter **reservations** in the consignment note.

### Article 9 — Evidential Value

The consignment note is **prima facie evidence** of the contract and conditions of carriage. If the carrier makes no reservations, it is presumed the goods were in apparent good order and condition on taking over.

## Carrier Liability — Article 17

### General Principle

The carrier is **liable for the total or partial loss of the goods** and for **damage** occurring between the time of taking over and delivery, and for **delay in delivery**.

### Exceptions to Liability (Article 17(2) and 17(4))

The carrier is exonerated if loss, damage or delay was caused by:
- **Wrongful act or neglect of the claimant**
- **Instructions of the claimant** not arising from a wrongful act or neglect of the carrier
- **Inherent vice** of the goods (e.g., natural deterioration)
- **Circumstances which the carrier could not avoid** and consequences which could not be prevented

**Special risks (Article 17(4) — partial exoneration where carrier proves contribution):**
- Use of open unsheeted vehicles when expressly agreed and stipulated
- Lack of or defective packing of goods
- Handling, loading, stowage or unloading by sender, consignee or their agents
- Inherent liability to wastage in bulk/weight, or inherent vice
- Insufficiency or inadequacy of marks or numbers on packages
- Carriage of livestock

### Article 18 — Burden of Proof

When the carrier establishes that loss or damage could have arisen from one of the special risks (Article 17(4)), it is **presumed** that it did so arise. The claimant may rebut this presumption.

## Limitation of Liability — Article 23

### Cargo Loss or Damage

Compensation for loss or damage is limited to:

**8.33 SDR (Special Drawing Rights) per kilogram of gross weight short**

This applies to the gross weight of the goods lost or damaged.

*Note: Before the 1978 Protocol, the limit was expressed in gold francs (25 NF/kg). The SDR replacement entered force in 1980 for states that have acceded to the 1978 Protocol.*

### Additional Costs Recoverable

In addition to the per-kg compensation, the following are recoverable:
- **Carriage charges** (freight)
- **Customs duties**
- **Other charges incurred** in respect of the carriage

These are refunded in full if goods are totally lost; proportionally if partially lost.

### Article 24 — Declared Value

If the sender declares in the consignment note a value exceeding the Article 23 limit and pays a surcharge, **compensation is not limited** to 8.33 SDR/kg — full declared value is recoverable.

### Article 25 — Compensation for Damage

For damage (not loss), compensation must not exceed:
- The amount payable for **total loss** if the whole consignment has been depreciated by the damage
- The amount payable for the **part affected** if only part of the consignment has been depreciated

### Article 26 — Special Interest in Delivery

The sender may declare a special interest in delivery, in case of loss or damage or of the agreed time limit being exceeded. If this is declared and a surcharge paid, compensation up to the declared amount is available beyond the normal limits.

---

## Delay in Delivery — Article 19

**Delay** occurs when goods have not been delivered within the agreed time limit, or — if no time limit was agreed — when the actual duration of carriage **exceeds the time it would be reasonable to allow** a diligent carrier.

### Compensation for Delay (Article 23(5))

In case of delay, compensation may not exceed **the carriage charges** (freight amount). There is no per-kg calculation for delay alone.

### Combined Loss/Damage and Delay

Where both loss/damage and delay occur, the total compensation is subject to the relevant caps for both.

---

## Time Limits for Claims — Article 32

| Claim type | Limitation period |
|-----------|-------------------|
| General claims (loss, damage, delay) | **1 year** |
| Claims involving wilful misconduct or equivalent fault | **3 years** |

The limitation period runs from:
- **Partial loss or damage**: date of delivery
- **Total loss**: 30th day after agreed delivery date (or if no date, 60th day from taking over)
- **Delay**: 21 days from delivery

The limitation period is **suspended** during the period between written notice of claim and the carrier's written reply rejecting the claim.

---

## Jurisdiction — Article 31

Claimant may bring action in:
1. Courts of a country where:
   - The defendant is **ordinarily resident**, has principal place of business, or branch
   - The **goods were taken over** by the carrier, or
   - **Designated place of delivery** is located
2. Court agreed upon in writing by the parties (but only in countries where permitted)

**Arbitration:** Allowed if the arbitration clause requires the arbitrator to apply CMR (Article 33).

---

## Rights of Successive Carriers — Articles 34–40

Where carriage is performed by successive carriers, each carrier accepting the goods becomes a party to the contract of carriage for the leg it performs. The **second and subsequent carriers** assume responsibility from the moment they take over the goods and the consignment note.

Liability is apportioned among carriers according to where the loss/damage occurred. Where it cannot be determined, liability is shared in proportion to their share of the agreed freight.

## Practical Documentation for Compliance

### Mandatory Documents at Roadside (EU)

When performing international road freight in the EU, drivers must carry:
- CMR consignment note (second copy — accompanies the goods)
- Community licence (certified copy) per EU Regulation 1072/2009
- Driver attestation if applicable (non-EU driver)

### CMR as Proof of International Transport Preceding Cabotage

Under EU Regulation 1072/2009 and the Mobility Package, drivers performing **cabotage** must carry CMR documentation proving the preceding international delivery. The consignment note (CMR) for the international leg serves as this proof.

## eCMR — Electronic CMR Waybill

### Legal Basis
The **Additional Protocol to the CMR concerning the Electronic Consignment Note (e-CMR)**, opened for signature in Geneva on 20 February 2008, provides the legal framework for eCMR.

### Ratification Status (as of 2026)

Countries that have ratified eCMR Protocol:
- EU states: Belgium, Bulgaria, Czech Republic, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, France, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Luxembourg, Netherlands, Romania, Slovakia, Slovenia, Spain, Sweden
- Non-EU: Norway, Switzerland, Turkey, Russia, Belarus, Georgia, Ukraine (partial/pending)
- UK has ratified

**Not yet ratified:** Germany, Austria, Italy, Poland, Greece, Croatia (paper CMR still required for transport to/from these countries unless an alternative arrangement exists)

### eCMR Requirements

An eCMR is legally equivalent to a paper CMR if:
- The electronic system is accessible to all parties (sender, carrier, consignee, competent authorities)
- Data is **tamper-evident** — any modification or attempt to modify must be detectable
- An **audit trail** of all changes is maintained
- The eCMR must be available during the entire transport (accessible to enforcement authorities)

### GS1 eCMR Standard

GS1 has published an eCMR standard (GS1 XML/JSON) for interoperability between different transport management systems. The standard uses globally unique identifiers (GS1 GLN for parties, GSIN for shipments) to ensure traceability.

**Reference:** https://www.gs1.org/standards/eCMR

### Practical Advantages of eCMR

| Benefit | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| Real-time data access | Sender, carrier, recipient can track consignment status digitally |
| Reduced paperwork | No physical document handling at border crossings |
| Faster dispute resolution | Digital audit trail simplifies claims process |
| Integration | Can link to TMS, ERP, and customs systems |
| Environmental | Eliminates paper |

## Penalties

CMR itself does not prescribe penalties — enforcement is through national law. However:

- **Failure to carry CMR at roadside** (EU): can result in sanction fees under national law; in Sweden, **10,000 SEK** per missing document under Yrkestrafikförordning (2012:237)
- **Carrier liability:** Not carrying CMR does not itself create additional liability beyond what Art. 17 imposes — but loss of a signed consignment note removes the prima facie evidence of good condition at loading, weakening the carrier's position
- **False declarations on CMR:** Can constitute fraud; consignee who accepts delivery without reservation loses right to claim for apparent damage (Art. 30)
